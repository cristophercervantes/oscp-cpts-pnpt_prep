# Networking Cables

## Two categories of network cable

### Copper cables

The most common type. Made of multiple pairs of copper wires inside a plastic insulator. Data is transmitted by changing voltage between two ranges — the receiving device interprets these voltage changes as binary 1s and 0s.

The most common copper cables in networking are twisted pair cables:

|Cable|Notes|
|---|---|
|Cat 5|Older standard, mostly replaced|
|Cat 5e|Reduced crosstalk vs Cat 5, current common standard|
|Cat 6|Stricter specs, faster and more reliable, but more expensive and shorter max distance at high speeds|

**Crosstalk** — when an electrical pulse on one wire is accidentally picked up by an adjacent wire, causing the receiving end to misread data. This results in errors and retransmissions, which slow down the network. Cat 5e and Cat 6 reduce crosstalk through better internal wire arrangement.

From the outside, Cat 5, 5e, and 6 cables look nearly identical. The differences are internal.

### Fiber optic cables

Contain tiny glass tubes that carry pulses of light instead of electrical signals. The light pulses represent binary 1s and 0s.

Compared to copper:

- Faster data transfer
- Longer distances without data loss
- Immune to electromagnetic interference
- More expensive and more fragile

Fiber is most commonly found in data centers rather than homes or offices.

# 56 - Network Devices

## Hub — Layer 1

A hub connects multiple computers but broadcasts all traffic to every device connected to it. Each device has to decide for itself whether the incoming data was meant for it.

Problem: this creates a **collision domain** — a network segment where only one device can communicate at a time. If multiple devices try to send simultaneously, the signals interfere and everything has to wait and retry.

Result: slow, noisy network. Hubs are mostly a historical artifact today.

## Switch — Layer 2 (Data Link)

A switch also connects multiple computers but is far smarter than a hub. It inspects the Ethernet data being sent and delivers it only to the specific device it's intended for.

This eliminates or dramatically reduces collision domains — fewer retransmissions, higher throughput. Switches are the standard way to connect devices on a local network (LAN).

## Router — Layer 3 (Network)

A router connects different networks together and forwards traffic between them. It inspects IP data to determine where to send traffic.

Two types:

- **Home / small office routers** — simple routing tables, mainly forward traffic from a local network to the ISP
- **Core ISP routers** — the backbone of the internet. Handle massive traffic volume and complex routing decisions. Communicate with each other using **BGP (Border Gateway Protocol)** to find the most efficient paths for traffic

When you load a web page, your traffic may pass through dozens of routers across the internet before reaching its destination.

## Servers and clients

**Server** — any node that provides data to something requesting it. **Client** — the node requesting data.

Most nodes act as both at different times. A desktop computer is primarily a client (fetching data for the user) but might occasionally act as a server. An email server is primarily a server but acts as a client when it queries a DNS server.

The terms describe the primary role of a node, not an exclusive one.
