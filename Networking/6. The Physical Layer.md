# The Physical Layer

The physical layer's job is to move 1s and 0s from one end of a link to the other. Everything else — packets, frames, protocols — is built on top of this foundation.

A **bit** is the smallest unit of data a computer understands — a single 1 or a 0. All network traffic, whether it's a song stream, an email, or a bank transaction, is ultimately 1s and 0s traveling across the physical layer.

## How data travels over copper wire

A standard copper network cable carries a constant electrical charge when connected. Data is sent by varying the voltage of that charge — a process called **modulation** (specifically **line coding** in network contexts).

One voltage state = 0. Another state = 1. Through this simple technique, modern networks can move 10 billion 1s and 0s per second across a single cable.

## Twisted pair cables

The most common network cable type. Pairs of copper wires are twisted together because:

- The twisting protects against electromagnetic interference
- It reduces crosstalk from neighboring wire pairs

A standard Cat 6 cable contains 8 wires — 4 twisted pairs — inside a single jacket.

## Duplex vs simplex communication

**Simplex** — data flows in one direction only.

**Duplex** — data can flow in both directions. Twisted pair cables achieve this by dedicating one or two pairs for each direction.

**Full duplex** — both devices can send and receive at the same time simultaneously. This is normal operation.

**Half duplex** — both directions are possible, but only one device can communicate at a time. If a network link reports itself as half-duplex, it's usually a sign something is wrong with the connection.

# Ethernet Over Twisted Pair
## Where twisted pair fits in a typical home or office network

ISP → coaxial or fiber into the building → gateway modem → twisted pair Ethernet to router → twisted pair Ethernet to devices internally

Twisted pair (CAT) cables handle the internal wired connections. The same cable type can also carry telephone and television services.

## Why twisted pair is popular for LANs

- Multiple levels of protection against EMI, RFI, and crosstalk
- Affordable — low-interference options exist for home and small business use
- Thin, lightweight, easy to install and move
- Suitable transmission range for connections inside buildings
- Frequency range supports both data and voice/telephone

## Three types of twisted pair cable

**UTP — Unshielded Twisted Pair** Most common and least expensive. Basic protection against interference. Found in most business and home networks.

**STP — Shielded Twisted Pair** Used where EMI, RFI, or crosstalk are known problems. Uses braided aluminum and/or copper shielding around the four twisted pairs beneath the outer jacket.

**FTP — Foiled Twisted Pair** Similar use case as STP but uses a thin foil shield instead of braided shielding. STP and FTP labels are often used interchangeably.

STP and FTP shielding can also be combined in the same cable for extra protection. Individual twisted pairs can also be shielded separately within the cable to further reduce crosstalk — this is the most expensive option and is typically used in industrial environments with high EMI/RFI.

**SF/FTP** — shielded with foiled twisted pair — used in high-interference industrial environments.

## Straight-through (patch) cables

The primary type of Ethernet cable used in networks. Connects computers and routers to hubs and switches, and servers to switches.

Identified by comparing both ends — the color and stripe order of wires at pin positions 1–8 is identical on both ends.

How the pins are used:

|Device|Pins 1 & 2|Pins 3 & 6|
|---|---|---|
|Computers and routers|Orange — sending|Green — receiving|
|Hubs and switches|Green — sending|Orange — receiving|

100Base-T (common home networks) only uses orange and green pairs. Gigabit Ethernet can also use blue and brown pairs for Power over Ethernet (PoE).

# Crossover Cables

Crossover cables connect two devices of the same type directly to each other — computer to computer, switch to switch, router to router, etc.

As an IT support specialist, you might use a crossover cable to connect a laptop directly to an enterprise device (server, switch, router) to perform administrative tasks like updates, repairs, or accessing the management interface.

## Auto-MDI/MDIX — the modern replacement

Most newer enterprise devices can automatically detect the connection type and switch wires internally as needed. This technology (Auto-MDI/MDIX) eliminates the need for crossover cables on modern hardware. Crossover cables are mainly relevant for older network environments.

## How to identify a crossover cable

Compare both ends. On a straight-through cable, the wire colors are in the same order at both ends. On a crossover cable, the green and orange wires swap positions between the two ends.

|End|Pins 1 & 2|Pins 3 & 6|
|---|---|---|
|Endpoint 1|Green (sending)|Orange (receiving)|
|Endpoint 2|Orange (sending)|Green (receiving)|

The swap is necessary because both devices use the same pins for sending and receiving — the cable has to cross those signals over so each device's transmit connects to the other's receive.

Blue and brown wires do not cross over.

Straight-through cables use the T568B wiring scheme. Crossover cables use both T568A at one end and T568B at the other.

# Network Ports, Plugs, and Patch Panels

## RJ45 — the standard network plug

Twisted pair cables terminate with a plug that exposes the internal wires for connection. The most common plug in computer networking is the **RJ45 (Registered Jack 45)**. It connects to an RJ45 network port on a device.

## Network ports

Network ports are built directly into networking devices:

- **Switches** — many ports (their job is to connect many devices)
- **Servers and desktops** — typically one or two ports
- **Laptops, tablets, phones** — often none (wireless only)

## Port LEDs — troubleshooting at a glance

Most network ports have two small LEDs:

|LED|What it indicates|
|---|---|
|Link light|Solid on = cable properly connected, both devices powered on|
|Activity light|Flashing = data actively being transmitted|

On some switches, a single LED serves both functions and may also indicate link speed. Always check the hardware documentation for the specific device you're working with — port lights are a quick first diagnostic when troubleshooting connectivity.

## Patch panels

Network ports aren't always attached directly to a device. Wall ports and desk ports are common in offices. These ports connect via cables run through walls to a **patch panel**.

A patch panel is simply a container for the endpoints of many cable runs — it does no active work. From the patch panel, additional cables run to switches or routers to give the wall/desk ports network access.

Flow: device → wall/desk port → cable in wall → patch panel → cable → switch/router → network

# Cabling Tools

## Physical layer cabling tools

|Tool|What it does|
|---|---|
|**Crimper**|Plier-like tool that squeezes (crimps) wires into place — used when terminating cable ends with plugs|
|**Cable stripper**|Removes the protective rubber coating from cables to expose the internal wires before termination|
|**Wi-Fi analyzer**|Scans and measures Wi-Fi signal power and quality in an area — useful for diagnosing wireless coverage issues|
|**Toner probe**|A two-part tool for tracing cables. One device plugs into the cable, the probe emits a tone that gets louder as it gets closer to the connected device — used to find which cable is which inside a wall or patch panel|
|**Punch down tool**|Punches wires into punch down panels or jacks. Strip the protective covering first, then use the tool to seat each wire into its slot|
|**Cable tester**|Digitally measures cable integrity and compliance with cabling standards. Checks: attenuation, impedance, noise, near-end crosstalk (NEXT), attenuation-to-crosstalk ratio (ACR), and PowerSum NEXT|
|**Loopback plug**|Tests a port by connecting wires in a loop — traffic sent out is routed back into the same port, confirming the port is functional|
|**Network tap**|External device that copies network traffic for use in monitoring or analysis without interrupting the live connection|
