# Protecting Data in Motion or at Rest

We generate insane amounts of data every second — emails, videos, IoT devices, mobile banking — all of it flying across networks. The problem? Bad actors are always lurking. This chapter is basically: _here's why encryption matters, and here's how it works at a basic level._

## How We Got Here

- Computers used to be massive, expensive, and owned only by governments/universities
- 1980: 1 GB hard drive = ~$1.2 million. By 2010: ~$0.10/GB
- As tech got cheaper, everyone got online → more data → more risk

**internet milestones:**

- 1972 – Email invented (Ray Tomlinson)
- 1982 – TCP/IP formalized
- 1983 – DNS top-level domains (.com, .edu, .gov) created
- 1988 – Morris Worm. First major internet attack. Changed everything. Cybersecurity as a concept was born.

## Threat Landscape

Three main categories of attacks today:

**Social Engineering** – Manipulating people into giving up info. Relies on trust and deception, not technical exploits.

**Malware** – Viruses, rootkits, spyware, trojans, ransomware. Goal is usually unauthorized access or locking you out.

**DoS / DDoS** – Flooding a system with requests until it goes down. DDoS uses botnets (armies of compromised machines) to hit harder.

## Types of Data We're Protecting

- **PII** (Personally Identifiable Information) – bank records, SSNs, credit card info
- **Business data** – financial reports, employee records, trade secrets

Both need protection. Data leaks = reputation damage + legal trouble.

## Security Services (from X.800 framework)

X.800 is an ITU document that outlines what security should look like. The key services:

**Confidentiality** – Keep private data private. Encryption is the main tool here. Even if someone intercepts data, it's unreadable without the key.

**Integrity** – Data shouldn't be modified without authorization. Tools like _Tripwire_ baseline your files, hash them, and alert you if anything changes.

- How Tripwire works: flag files → generate hash baseline → periodically re-hash → compare → alert if mismatch

**Authentication** – Prove who you are. Message authentication codes verify the source of a message. Spoofed emails are a classic violation of this.

**Non-repudiation** – You can't deny sending/receiving something. Digital signatures handle this.

- _Proof of origin_ = message came from you
- _Proof of receipt_ = you received it
- Real talk: if Paul had signed that birthday party email with a digital signature, Bob wouldn't have taken the blame.

**Availability** – Resources are accessible to authorized users. DoS attacks violate this. You can't fix it with crypto — intrusion detection, patching, and upgrades are the answer here.

## Cryptographic Concepts

**Encryption** – Plaintext → ciphertext using an algorithm + key

**Hashing** – Any input → fixed-length output. One-way. Can't reverse it to get the original.

**Digital Signature** – Uses asymmetric encryption to verify authenticity + non-repudiation.

## Trusted Third Party (TTP) & PKI

When Alice shops at Kiddikatz online, how does she know she's actually talking to Kiddikatz and not some attacker (Mallory) in the middle?

**TTP works on transitive trust:**

> Alice trusts TTP → Kiddikatz trusts TTP → Alice trusts Kiddikatz

**PKI (Public Key Infrastructure)** – Manages this trust using digital certificates. It doesn't generate keys directly — it generates _certificates_ that securely distribute public keys.

Two types of encryption:

- **Symmetric** – One shared key for both sides
- **Asymmetric** – Public key (shared openly) + Private key (kept secret)

The padlock in your browser = PKI at work. Verified by companies like Cloudflare, Verisign, Thawte.

## Classic Encryption: Substitution & Transposition

**Substitution** – Replace each letter with a symbol/character according to a key

- Example: _Pigpen cipher_ – uses a grid of symbols to represent letters
- Simple, but breakable

**Transposition** – Rearrange the letters according to a pattern

- Example: _Rail fence (zig-zag) cipher_ – write text across multiple "rails" diagonally
- Also breakable if you know to look for patterns

## Breaking the Code

**Letter Frequency Analysis** – Works on mono-alphabetic ciphers (one alphabet used)

- High-frequency letters in English: A, E, T
- Low-frequency: K, Q, X, Z
- Common digrams: th, he, of, it
- Common trigrams: the, est, and, for, his

If a cipher uses multiple alphabets, frequency analysis becomes much harder.
