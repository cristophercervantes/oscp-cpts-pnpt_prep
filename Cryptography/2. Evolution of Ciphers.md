# The Evolution of Ciphers

Humans have been hiding information for thousands of years. Most of that effort came from **war** — keeping secrets from the enemy. Over time, these methods evolved from physical tricks to mathematical systems to digital encryption.

## Early Uses of Cryptography

### Tattoos (6th century BCE)

One of the strangest methods ever: Greek ruler **Histiaeus** wanted to send a secret message to start a revolt against the Persian king Darius I.

His plan:

1. Shave a trusted servant's scalp
2. Tattoo the message (`REVOLT`) on the head
3. Wait for hair to grow back
4. Send the servant to the ally, Aristagoras
5. Aristagoras shaves the servant's head → reads the message

This is an early example of **steganography** — hiding _that_ a message exists, not just its contents.

### Scytale (7th century BCE)

Used by the **Greeks and Spartans**. A physical transposition cipher.

**How it worked:**

|Step|Who|Action|
|---|---|---|
|Encrypt|Sender|Wrap parchment around wooden rod → write message across one line → fill rest with random characters|
|Decrypt|Receiver|Wrap parchment around _identical_ rod → message appears|

Without the matching rod? Just meaningless letters on a strip.

> his is called a **transposition cipher** — it rearranges letters rather than replacing them.

### Monoalphabetic Ciphers

A **monoalphabetic substitution cipher** uses a single alphabet and swaps one letter for another using a key.

#### The Caesar Cipher (~50 BCE)

Created by **Julius Caesar** to keep communications private. It shifts every letter by a fixed number of spaces.

**Example — shift by 2:**

```
Plain:  A B C D E F G
Cipher: F G A B C D E  (wait — actually shifts forward)

A → C
B → D
...and so on
```

To decode: shift each letter _back_ by the same amount.

**Why it worked at the time:**

- You couldn't decode it without knowing the shift number
- Scholars estimate only **10–20% of the population** could even read — which added another layer of security

**Weakness:** Vulnerable to **letter frequency analysis** — in any language, some letters appear far more often than others (e.g., E in English). Patterns give the cipher away.

### Polyalphabetic Ciphers

To defeat frequency analysis, cryptographers added **more than one alphabet** to the mix.

In **1563**, Giovanni Battista Porta published _The Secret of Letters_, introducing the **Porta Cipher** — a polyalphabetic cipher.

#### The Vigenère Cipher (1586)

Developed by **Blaise de Vigenère**. Uses a 26×26 table (the Vigenère square) where each row is shifted one step further.

**How to encrypt:**

```
Phrase:  L E T S S T U D Y C R Y P T O G R A P H Y
Key:     T I G E R K I T T E N S T I G E R K I T T
         (key repeats until it matches phrase length)
```

Each letter of the phrase is looked up where it intersects with the corresponding key letter in the table.

```
L + T → E
E + I → M
...and so on
```

Result: `EMZWJDCWRGEQIBUKIKXAR` — nearly impossible to crack without knowing the key.

## Encoding Transmissions During War

Communication during conflict = life or death. Military leaders needed fast, secure channels to coordinate troops.

### Methods Over Time

|Era|Method|
|---|---|
|Ancient|Riders on horseback, homing pigeons, messenger dogs|
|Pre-modern|Signal flags, cannon blasts, coordinated lights|
|Modern|Telegraph, telephone, radio|

### Morse Code

Invented by **Samuel F.B. Morse** before the American Civil War. Sent electrical signals over copper wire.

**Encoding rules:**

- 3 dots = 1 dash
- Space within a letter = 1 dot
- Space between letters = 3 dots
- Space between words = 7 dots

Transformed battlefield communication by enabling **near-instant** long-distance messaging.
### Flash Signaling

A visual version of Morse code using **long and short flashes of light**. The **heliograph** (two mirrors capturing sunlight) was one device used — though it required sunlight, limiting its range.

### Telephone & Radio

- **Telephone** (1876, Alexander Graham Bell) — phone lines were strung on poles or buried in trenches. Risk: lines could be cut or destroyed by artillery.
- **Radio** (late 1800s) — wireless, no wires to cut. But new risk: **anyone could intercept the signal**.

This interception threat drove the need for stronger encryption.

### The Enigma Machine (WWII)

Developed by German engineer **Arthur Scherbius** after WWI. A mechanical encryption device with nearly unbreakable codes.

**Components:**

|Part|Function|
|---|---|
|**Rotors** (5 total, 3 used at once)|Could be adjusted/rotated to change encoding|
|**Keyboard** (26 keys)|Letters only — no spaces, numbers, or punctuation|
|**Lampboard**|Lit up to show the encoded letter|
|**Plugboard (Steckers)**|26 sockets; wires swapped letter pairs (e.g., Y ↔ T) for extra complexity|

German troops used the Enigma throughout WWII to encode strategic messages. It was eventually cracked by mathematician **Alan M. Turing**.

## Entering the Digital Age

As computers spread through government and industry in the **1960s–70s**, protecting digital data became urgent.

### Claude Shannon — The Foundation

In **1941**, Claude Shannon was a scientist at **Bell Labs**, researching Boolean algebra and circuit switching. He also worked on classified military cryptography projects.

His landmark paper:

> _A Mathematical Theory of Cryptography_ (written 1941, classified; declassified and published **1949**)

This paper **established cryptography as a science**.

#### Shannon's Two Core Principles

|Concept|What It Means|
|---|---|
|**Confusion**|Obscure any connection between plaintext, ciphertext, and the key|
|**Diffusion**|Spread the influence of the plaintext/key across as much of the ciphertext as possible|

These are achieved through repeated rounds of:

- **Substitution** — swap one bit/character for another (using an **S-BOX / lookup table**)
- **Permutation** — rearrange the order of bits/characters (e.g., using a **circular shift**)

#### Circular Shift Example

**Left circular shift by 2:**

```
Input:  00010111
Output: 01011100
(2 bits removed from left, appended to right)
```

**Right circular shift by 2:**

```
Input:  00010011
Output: 11000100
(2 bits removed from right, appended to left)
```

### The Feistel Cipher (1973)

At IBM, scientist **Horst Feistel** developed a **block cipher** structure based on Shannon's principles.

- Not a specific cipher — it's a **template/blueprint** for building block ciphers
- Uses **successive, repeatable rounds** of substitution and permutation on each block of data
- Foundation for most modern block ciphers, including **DES**

### Lucifer & DES

Feistel and his IBM colleagues used their structure to build the **Lucifer cipher**:

- Used a **128-bit key**
- Encrypted **128-bit blocks** of text
- Direct precursor to the **Data Encryption Standard (DES)**
